Building Your First Chicken Coop on a Budget

A useful chicken coop starts with dry ground, ventilation, enough space, secure wire and a door that closes properly. The figures below are an example worksheet, not a quote: material prices and suitable construction methods vary by location and site conditions.

This guide uses a small coop measuring 2m x 1.5m as a planning example. Its 3 square meters of floor area should not be described as housing 15–20 adult hens. Using a planning allowance of three adult birds per square meter gives space for about nine birds before accounting for equipment or other obstructions. Plan for fewer birds if the usable floor area, heat or bird size requires it, and provide suitable daytime exercise space. Secure construction reduces predator risk, but the materials and design need to suit your site.

What You Will Need

Illustrative materials budget: UGX 80,000 – 100,000. This is a planning example, not a current supplier quotation. Timber or roofing materials you already own can reduce the cash you need. List those separately, choose either a suitable metal or thatched roof, and obtain current prices for the remaining materials, transport and labour.

Step-by-Step Construction

1 Choose the Right Location

Select a spot that is:

  • Well-drained: Avoid low-lying areas where water collects during the rainy season.
  • Shaded: Partial shade from a tree helps keep the coop cool in Uganda's hot afternoons.
  • Elevated: Build on slightly raised ground to prevent flooding.
  • Accessible: You will visit the coop twice daily, so place it near your house.
Check the site: Nearby walls, branches and fences can provide climbing routes. Inspect the roof, door, mesh and ground-level gaps together; a fixed separation distance alone does not make a coop secure.

2 Build the Frame

Mark out a rectangular frame measuring approximately 2m x 1.5m. Ask a local builder to confirm suitable post sizes, bracing and foundation depth for your soil, wind exposure and roof weight. Set the supports firmly and check the frame is stable before adding the walls and roof.

Timber and soil: Do not coat coop timber with used engine oil or unidentified leftover paint. Used motor oil can contain toxic substances and contaminate soil and water. Choose durable materials and good drainage, and ask a local building or timber specialist about protection suitable for an animal enclosure. Follow the label of any product used and prevent contact with birds, feed and drinking water.

3 Add the Walls

Attach chicken wire mesh to three sides of the frame using nails or U-nails. The fourth side will be your door. For the lower 60cm of the walls, use solid boards or thick papyrus mats to block wind and provide privacy for the nesting area.

4 Build the Roof

Create a sloped roof using iron sheets or thatched grass. The slope should angle toward the back so rainwater drains away from the entrance. A sloped roof also helps reflect heat — keeping the interior cooler during hot days.

If using iron sheets, place a layer of papyrus or dry grass underneath. This provides insulation and reduces the noise of rain hitting the metal.

5 Create the Nesting Boxes

Build three nesting boxes along one wall, each measuring 30cm x 30cm x 40cm. Place them 30cm above the floor and add a small perch in front so hens can easily enter. Line each box with dry grass or soft cloth.

Rule of Thumb: Provide one nesting box for every 4–5 hens. Two or three boxes can serve a small flock of around eight or nine hens. Account for any nesting boxes that reduce usable floor space when deciding how many birds to keep.

6 Add Perches (Roosting Bars)

Install two horizontal perches at different heights (40cm and 60cm from the floor). Use smooth, round poles about 5cm in diameter. Space them 30cm apart horizontally. Chickens prefer to sleep on perches — it keeps them off the damp floor and reduces parasite exposure.

7 Build the Door and Secure the Coop

Construct a simple door using timber frame and wire mesh. Attach it with hinges and add a secure latch. Predators in Uganda — especially dogs and genets — are clever. Use a latch that requires a sliding motion, not just a hook.

8 Prepare the Floor

Spread a 10cm layer of dry grass, wood shavings, or chopped banana leaves on the floor. This bedding absorbs moisture and makes cleaning easier. Replace the bedding every 2–3 weeks and compost the old material for your garden.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Making the coop too small: Use usable floor area, not just the outside dimensions, to plan capacity. FAO gives three hens per square meter as a floor-housing example. At that planning rate, a clear 3-square-meter floor allows about nine adult birds; equipment, larger birds and local conditions can reduce that number. Give birds more space where possible and review the layout with a local poultry adviser before expanding.
2. Poor ventilation: A completely enclosed coop traps heat and ammonia. Always leave gaps near the roof for air circulation, covered with wire mesh to keep predators out.
3. Neglecting the door latch: A loose latch can expose the flock to predators. Check the closure each evening and choose a fastening that cannot be pushed open easily.

Upgrading Your Coop Over Time

As your flock grows, upgrade the coop incrementally:

  • Month 1–3: Basic coop as described above.
  • Month 4–6: Add a covered run area where chickens can move freely during the day.
  • Month 7–12: Install a rainwater collection system for automatic water supply.
  • Before expanding: Increase suitable housing space before adding more birds. Plan any separate housing around the age and management needs of your actual flock.

Sources and corrections

The floor-space planning example is based on FAO's Small-scale poultry production: General Management. It is a planning reference, not a guarantee that one stocking density suits every climate, bird type or housing system.

For the contamination risks of used motor oil, see the US Environmental Protection Agency's guidance on managing and recycling used oil.
